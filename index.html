How much warmer, wetter or drier could it get? See what a family of climate models projects for a place under each scenario, and how far they agree.

    Loading the emulator…

    What this is. A JavaScript port of METEOR's generation kernel, driven by a compact bundle exported from a model trained offline on CMIP6 output. The whole payload is about 300 KB and there is no server: the emulator runs on your machine.

    What the range shows. Each climate model gives a somewhat different answer, and the chart shows how much they agree: the line is the middle model, the darker band the middle half of the models, and the lighter band the middle 90%. These are the models' long-term responses to each scenario, without the ups and downs of individual years, measured from each model's own 1850–1900 level. Precipitation change is a percentage of that level. The Expert view shows year-to-year variability, one model at a time, with maps, other baselines and more.

    Places. The 58 AR6 reference regions, and 252 cities: every national capital of more than half a million people, and every other city of more than 2.5 million. A city is the nearest gridbox of each model that is more than half land, one to three degrees across, so it describes the city's surroundings rather than its streets.

    Two generations of scenario. The CMIP6 SSPs and the CMIP7 ScenarioMIP markers were built years apart and harmonized to different vintages of history, so they are not interchangeable. Both are driven here through the same simple climate model, CICERO-SCM, which is what makes comparing them meaningful at all: taking CMIP7's forcing from its own MAGICC assessment instead would have put a 0.74 W/m² model difference inside what looks like a scenario difference.

    Baselines. Temperature, and the maps, are shown as change from a reference period: 1850–1900, the pre-industrial convention warming levels are defined against, or 2005–2024, recent history. Either is the model's own forced response averaged over the period under CMIP7 Medium, and every scenario shares it, so the difference between two scenarios does not depend on the choice. Measuring from recent history removes the part of the spread between models that each inherited from its own historical warming — the models here warmed by between 0.7 and 1.6 °C from 1850–1900 to 2005–2024 — so switching baselines is a quick way to see how much of their disagreement about the future is disagreement about the past. The precipitation timeseries stays in absolute mm/day.

    Degree days. Heating and cooling degree days, base 18 °C, count how far daily temperatures fall below or rise above the point where buildings need heating or cooling. They start from the observed climate of 1995–2014 (W5E5, daily) and add each model's warming for each month, so they do not inherit a model's biases in absolute temperature or in how much days vary. For a region they average its land gridboxes' degree days; for a city they use the nearest land gridbox of the observations, half a degree across. They are not offered for the sea or the global mean.

    What it is not. The band around any one model is that model's internal variability — not model uncertainty, and not the full uncertainty in a projection. Compare models to see how much they disagree; with the 2005–2024 baseline, that is disagreement about the future rather than about the past. The difference between two models' maps is taken on the first model's grid, the second interpolated onto it. Maps show the forced response only; variability at a region you draw yourself would need a further 11 MB of EOF maps that this page does not load. Custom emissions need a simple climate model, which is not here either, so the scenarios on offer are the ones the bundle was built with. Precipitation covers 2015–2100 only, the window its distribution transform was fitted for.
